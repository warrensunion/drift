About the science

Simulation with uncertainty, not a forecast.

Everything on the globe is built from real measurements: satellite-derived currents, satellite winds, and thousands of drifting buoys like the one on the right, a float at the surface tied to a drogue 15 m down. Here is what went in, what came out, and where it falls short.

The data

Currents
Monthly averages of the OSCAR surface currents, which are derived from satellites, over 2011 to 2014, on a grid of 1° cells.
Wind
Monthly averages of QuikSCAT satellite winds at 10 m, 1999 to 2009, with gaps near coasts and sea ice filled from the nearest open water.
Drifters
The Global Drifter Program’s buoys, 2012 to 2014, one position a day. Some lost their drogue and rode at the surface, catching more wind; the model uses that difference.
Land
Natural Earth coastlines.

The model

A small neural network reads ten numbers about a spot in the ocean: where it is, that month’s average current and wind, the time of year, and how much the object catches the wind. It predicts how far a drifter moves over the next day, east and north, together with how unsure it is. On the globe, every fragment is a cloud of copies, each moved by that guess plus its own share of the uncertainty.

Its limits

  • Climatology, not live. Currents and winds are monthly averages over a few years. No particular storm or eddy is in them.
  • A coarse grid. Cells are 1° across, about 111 km at the equator, so coasts, straits and anything smaller are blurred.
  • Windage is a proxy. The drifter record only covers objects that slip downwind at about 0.1 % to 1 % of the wind speed, so a bottle riding high and a flat bag look the same to the model.
  • Fragmentation timing is uncertain. No study has timed how long a floating item takes to break into smaller pieces. Drift’s rates are assumptions built from lab, beach and seafloor measurements.
  • Flat water. Sinking, beaching and gathering in gyres follow simple rules with cited rates; nothing moves in depth.
